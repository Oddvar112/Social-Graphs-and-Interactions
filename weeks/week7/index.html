Week 7 · From meaning to measurement

Say it in other words and keyword search hears one word, often somebody else's name.

The course's last opener this week: find queries where sparse and dense retrieval disagree. We made the disagreement on purpose. For 61 Marvel characters we wrote two clues before computing any ranking for them, one in the words of the character's own Wikipedia page and one with the same facts in other words, and then let two engines rank all 303 pages for every clue: KEYWORD, which is week 6's TF-IDF, and MEANING, the course's MiniLM sentence encoder reading 180-word passages. In the page's own words they are close to a tie. In other words KEYWORD finds the right page in its top three 6 times out of 61 and MEANING 24 times. When KEYWORD misses, one word carries the wrong answer, a median 96 percent of its score, and in 32 of 58 misses that word is part of the wrong character's name: gun and kid sent six clues to the Two-Gun Kid. We also described six characters who have no page. Both engines answered anyway, and MEANING was about as sure of Hercules being Thor as it was of its right answers. Then we turned it into a game show.

THE SHORT VERSION
  • We wrote the questions first. 61 characters with two clues each (the page's own words, and other words), plus six characters who are not in the corpus: 128 clues, checked for leaked page vocabulary and rewritten before the first ranking, and not touched after it.
  • In the page's own words it is a tie. The right page is in the top three for 54 of 61 clues under KEYWORD and 57 under MEANING; first for 38 and 42.
  • In other words, KEYWORD goes deaf. Top three: 6 of 61, median rank 13. MEANING: 24 of 61, median rank 5. For the same character, KEYWORD did worse with other words 57 times out of 61 and never better.
  • One word, often a name. In 56 of KEYWORD's 58 misses on other words, one word of the clue carries at least half of the wrong page's score, median 96 percent, and in 32 that word is in the wrong page's title: gun to Two-Gun Kid, red to Red Wolf, girl to Spider-Girl, bullet to Blue Bullet. Week 6 again: a name is the strongest signal TF-IDF has.
  • MEANING matches meaning. Where it finds the page from other words, the passage it matched contains a median 8 percent of the clue's distinctive words. 32 of the 61 other-words clues use words that no page uses at all (litigator, courtroom, chrysalis); KEYWORD cannot see them, MEANING can.
  • We read all 45 of MEANING's misses. 7 are the right character's story on somebody else's page, 7 are lookalikes, 8 take one word literally (speed demon finds a passage about demons), 14 share a theme and nothing else, 9 are noise.
  • Nobody here. Asked about Thor, KEYWORD says Beta Ray Bill and MEANING says Hercules. To answer "nobody" to all six missing characters, MEANING would have to refuse every answer it scores 0.535 or lower, and that throws away 22 of its 58 right answers, 15 of the 16 it found from other words.
  • A machine can learn to say nobody, a little. Not from its score but from how far its number one stands out from the other 302 pages. Judged on clues the rule was not fitted to, MiniLM then says nobody to 3 of the 5 missing characters the game deals, at the cost of 6 right answers; the biggest encoder we tried says it to 2 and gives up none.
  • Smaller passages help. Other words, top three: 60-word passages 32 of 61, 180-word passages 24, one averaged vector per page 18, the first 256 word pieces 17.
  • A bigger encoder helps where it matters. We ran five larger sentence encoders on the same clues. The largest, mxbai-embed-large, finds the page first for 25 of the other-words clues (MiniLM 16) and has it in its top three for 43 (MiniLM 24). On the page's own words all six are within a few clues of KEYWORD.
  • IN OTHER WORDS, the game. Ten clues an episode against both engines, portraits, a clock, a NOBODY HERE buzzer, one lifeline per machine, and an instant replay of the words and passages each machine went on. In the game MEANING is mxbai-embed-large, and both machines can say NOBODY HERE.

 What we asked

  1. When does searching by meaning beat searching by words? Measured, not anecdotal: the same facts, two wordings, two engines.
  2. What does each engine hear when it is wrong? The word that carries a keyword miss; the passage behind a meaning miss, read.
  3. What happens when the answer is not there? Six characters with no page.
  4. Which choices matter? Passage length, one vector per page, and what the encoder silently cuts off.

I.Writing the questions first

A retrieval test is only as honest as its queries, and the easiest way to make a method look good is to write the queries after seeing what it finds. So the clues came first. We picked 61 characters, most of them among the best-known pages (the ones most pages link to) and a handful of obscure ones we knew well enough to describe, all with a portrait. For each we wrote two clues with the same facts:

  • In the page's own words. Distinctive words that are on the character's Wikipedia page, but never the character's own name. Other characters, teams and places appear, as they do on the page. "A physicist exposed to gamma radiation who transforms into a green-skinned brute of limitless strength when angry."
  • In other words. The same facts, written to avoid the page's rare words: at most one word that is on the page and on 30 or fewer of the 303 pages. "A mild-mannered researcher who becomes an enormous emerald giant whenever he is provoked, after being caught in the explosion of a test weapon."

And six clues about characters who have no page in the corpus at all: Thor, Captain America, Iron Man, Daredevil, the Silver Surfer and Magneto. Week 5's search engine could not find Thor because he is not there; this week we wanted to see what an engine says instead.

Then a vocabulary check (which of each clue's words are on its page, and on how many pages), two rounds of rewriting the other-words clues that leaked, and only then the first ranking. Nothing was edited after it. Two clues still carry two rare page words each (Black Cat's blonde and famous, Storm's pickpocket and Egypt): the check that found them ran in the same command as the first ranking, so they stay as written and count as they are. In the end, of the 61 other-words clues, 24 share no rare word with their page, 35 share one and 2 share two. One more thing we noticed only afterwards: the corpus has the Ultimate Marvel version of Iron Man, and our Iron Man clue describes him too. It stays in every number below, flagged; the game does not deal it.

II.Two engines

KEYWORD is week 6's machine: every page a TF-IDF vector (tf = count / words on the page, idf = ln(303 / pages with the word), unit length), the clue weighted the same way, and the score the cosine between the two. A clue word that no page uses has no idf; it does not exist for the engine. That is the whole of vocabulary mismatch in one sentence.

MEANING is the course's encoder, all-MiniLM-L6-v2: a text in, 384 numbers out, unit length, and the score a dot product. It reads at most 256 word pieces, and 302 of the 303 pages are longer (the median page is 1,644 pieces), so encoding a whole page silently keeps its first few paragraphs. We did what the course page does: cut every page into passages of at most 180 words at sentence boundaries (4,479 passages; the course page has 4,473, our sentence splitter differs a little), encode every passage, and score a page by its best passage. As a check, the course's own example query, a Russian spy who became an Avenger, comes out the same: with one averaged vector per page Red Guardian is first and Black Widow 21st (the course page says 20th); scored by the best passage she is second, behind Red Guardian.

Both engines rank all 303 pages for every clue. What we record is the rank of the right page.

III.The price of paraphrase

In the page's own words the two engines are close: KEYWORD puts the right page first for 38 of 61 clues and in the top three for 54; MEANING 42 and 57. They tie on rank in 33 of the 61. Change the words and keep the facts, and KEYWORD's median rank goes from 1 to 13: for 57 of the 61 characters the right page drops, for the other 4 it stays, and it never rises. MEANING's median goes from 1 to 5, and 24 of the 61 stay in the top three. Head to head on the other-words clues, MEANING ranks the right page higher 43 times, KEYWORD 16 times, and twice they tie.

That is the dense-retrieval story the course tells, and it holds. The rest of the post is about the part we did not expect: what each engine hears when it gets it wrong.

IV.Why KEYWORD misses: one word, often a name

A TF-IDF cosine is a sum with one term per word (week 6's whole method), so for every miss we can ask which word carried the wrong page. On the other-words clues, KEYWORD's number one is wrong 58 times. In 56 of them one word of the clue carries at least half of that page's score, and the median is 96 percent: the wrong answer is one word, not a reading. In 32 of the 58, that word is part of the wrong page's own title.

The last two are not names, and they work the same way: snow is on 12 pages and counts most on Jack Frost's, and Jade is a villain on Anya Corazon's page. The Two-Gun Kid wins six clues, more than any other page, on gun four times and kid twice. The mechanism is week 6's: a word that is part of a character's name is repeated all over that character's page, so its TF-IDF weight there is large, and a paraphrase that avoids the right page's vocabulary leaves that one repeated word to decide. Names are the strongest signal TF-IDF has, and when the right words are missing, it finds a name in the wrong ones.

The same thing happens in the page's own words, more quietly. Spider-Man's clue (a teenager bitten by a radioactive spider …) puts Radioactive Man first: spider is on 146 of the 303 pages, because everybody links to Spider-Man, so it weighs almost nothing, and radioactive is on 20. Of KEYWORD's 23 misses on page-words clues, in 12 the word that counts most is part of the wrong page's name: Wolverine in Jubilee's clue, the Hulk in the Abomination's, venom blasts in Spider-Woman's.

And the other half of vocabulary mismatch is the words KEYWORD cannot hear at all. 32 of the 61 other-words clues contain at least one word that no page uses (46 words: courtroom, litigator, judges, chrysalis, bayou, shambles, heists, spangled); 1 of the 61 page-words clues does. With no rare page word left in the clue, KEYWORD found the right page in its top three once in 24 tries.

V.How MEANING wins, and how it misses

MEANING finds the right page first for 16 of the 61 other-words clues. In those 16, the passage it matched contains a median 8 percent of the clue's distinctive words (16 of 149 in total): it is not matching words. Two of them, side by side with the clue:

Clue: a courtroom litigator who shared her famous relative's blood … argues in front of judges as a seven-foot jade woman.
She-Hulk's passage (0.444): … She-Hulk is also a skilled and experienced attorney who attended the UCLA School of Law … had the power to compel her to adjudicate cases anywhere in creation.
Clue: a pair of kids who ran away from home, were drugged by criminals and came out as a dark teleporter and a girl who throws glowing blades.
Cloak and Dagger's passage (0.473): … The two teens would end up being forcibly kidnapped … a criminal chemist who's developing a new synthetic drug … with tests on runaway teens …

Not one of the clue's distinctive words is in either passage. Three of She-Hulk's (courtroom, litigator, judges) are on no page at all, so KEYWORD never saw them; the encoder was trained on far more text than these 303 pages, and still put the clue next to an attorney. In the second pair the clue says ran away and drugged and the page says runaway and drug: to a bag of words those are different words, to the encoder they are the same story.

MEANING's number one is wrong for the other 45, and we read every one of the passages it matched (they are in data/week7_meaning_misses.tsv, with a label and a line each). We sorted them into five kinds:

  • The right story on somebody else's page (7). For Black Widow it found the KGB training her husband in secret and hiding it from Natasha, on Red Guardian's page; for Emma Frost, her school and one of her students trying to read her mind, on Empath's; for Doctor Strange, a passage on Doctor Druid's page that names him. The retriever found text about the character. It is the wrong page.
  • Lookalikes (7). Different characters who share at least two of the clue's facts: Stingray, a man who designed and wears an armoured battlesuit, for War Machine; Gargoyle, a Soviet scientist and the Hulk's first foe, for the Abomination; the Two-Gun Kid's black suit, black hat and revolvers for Spider-Man Noir.
  • One word taken literally (8). Quicksilver is a speed demon, so a passage about Hell-lords. The Werewolf changes when the moon is full, so a passage about spies living on the Moon. Killraven fights the invaders from The War of the Worlds, so a passage about the superhero team called the Invaders. KEYWORD made the same mistake on the same word.
  • One theme and nothing specific (14). A fighter for Wolverine, a burglary for Black Cat, a meteorite for Wakanda's miracle ore.
  • Noise (9). Nothing we could connect to the clue.

So half of MEANING's misses (22 of 45) are wrong in a way a person can follow, and the replay in the game shows the passage every time. That is the useful property of a dense retriever that the accuracy number hides: its mistakes are near the meaning.

KEYWORD's answers are word matches again: ice and frozen for Captain America, herald for the Silver Surfer (Firelord was a herald of Galactus too, which is almost right). Its Iron Man answer is the Ultimate Marvel Iron Man, the case we flagged in section I: the clue describes him as well, and we count it as neither right nor wrong. MEANING's answers are more interesting. For Captain America, Magneto and Thor it found passages that mention the missing character, on somebody else's page. For a system that hands passages to a generator, that is the right text. As an answer to "who is this?", it is the wrong person, said with confidence.

How much confidence: MEANING's six answers about nobody scored 0.42 to 0.54, and its right answers have a median of 0.56. To say "nobody here" to all six, it would have to refuse every answer that scores 0.535 or lower, which throws away 22 of its 58 right answers, including 15 of the 16 it found from other words. The answers it gets right from a paraphrase are exactly the ones it is least sure of. KEYWORD's scores are no better as a detector: refusing everything at or below its highest score for a missing character (0.112) throws away 10 of its 41 right answers, all 3 of its other-words ones. The score is a similarity, not a probability that the page is right. It cannot say that the answer is not in the corpus.

Can a machine learn to say it? Not from the score, but a little from the shape of the whole ranking. When the character is there, the right page tends to stand out from the other 302; when nobody is there, the number one is merely the best of a flat crowd. So we measured how far each engine's number one stands out, as z = (best − mean) / standard deviation of all 303 scores, or as the gap to the runner-up in the same units, and let the engine say nobody below a threshold. The threshold is the one that scores best in the game (an episode of 4 clues in the page's own words, 5 in other words and 1 about a missing character; the five missing characters the game deals, without Iron Man), and since a threshold fitted on the clues it is judged on flatters itself, every clue is judged by a threshold fitted without it. MiniLM with the z rule says nobody to 3 of the 5 and gives up 6 right answers; KEYWORD, whose best measure is the gap, says nobody to 1 and gives up 3. Neither gains much: 4.07 points an episode becomes 4.19 for MiniLM, and KEYWORD stays at 2.7. The larger encoder in the next section does better: 2 of the 5, and no right answer lost.

Out of 61 clues of each kind. Shorter passages help: with 60 words, 32 of the other-words clues have the right page in the top three. Moon Knight's clue (a hired gun left for dead in Egypt) is the cleanest example: his page says, nearly word for word, "the mercenary who left Marc Spector for dead in Egypt", but in a 180-word passage that sentence shares the vector with several other villains, and the page ranks 15th; in a 60-word passage it is third, and averaged over the whole page it is 194th. Averaging a long page blurs every particular fact into the page's general drift. Cutting at 256 word pieces does surprisingly well on the page's own words (42 first), probably because the first paragraphs of a Wikipedia article are its summary and our page-words clues lean on them; with other words it falls to 17 in the top three. The conclusions survive every change: meaning beats words on paraphrase, and the two are close on the page's own words. And how a page is cut up moves the other-words top three between 17 and 32, about as far as the choice of engine moves it.

The bigger encoders gain most where MiniLM was weakest: with other words the largest finds the right page first for 25 of the 61 clues and has it in its top three for 43, where MiniLM had 16 and 24. In the page's own words all of them are within a few clues of KEYWORD (37 to 50 first, against 38), so the post's main finding holds for every encoder: when the clue shares the page's words, a bag of words is as good as a neural network, and when it does not, it is not close. With 60-word passages the best are bge-base (52 and 24 first, 5.4 points an episode) and mxbai (50 and 27, 5.5). The NOBODY HERE rule from section VI, judged on clues it was not fitted to, adds 0.4 points to mxbai with 180-word passages (5.5: nobody to 2 of the 5 missing characters, no right answer given up) and nothing to it with 60-word passages, where no threshold separates the missing characters from the present ones. That is why the game plays mxbai with 180-word passages. Everything here is in data/week7_encoders.json, written by scripts/compare_week7_encoders.py.

VIII.So we made it a game show

IN OTHER WORDS is the test with you in it. The host reads a clue; six portraits flip in; you point at one, or hit NOBODY HERE. KEYWORD and MEANING play the same clue at the same time against all 303 pages and lock in before you have finished reading (the six portraits include their number ones, and the rest are pages one of them ranked highly, so every decoy is plausible to a machine). An episode is ten clues: four in the page's own words, five in other words, one about somebody who is not there. Each machine will show you its top three and its answer once an episode, which turns out to be the interesting decision: trust KEYWORD when the clue sounds like Wikipedia, MEANING when it does not, and neither when the clue is about Thor.

In the game, MEANING is mxbai-embed-large rather than MiniLM: with MiniLM the machines were easy to beat, about 4 points in 10 an episode. And both machines can say NOBODY HERE, by the rule from section VI with its threshold fitted on the clues the game deals: KEYWORD when its number one is less than 1.71 standard deviations ahead of the runner-up, MEANING when its number one stands less than 2.75 above the average page. On these clues that has MEANING say nobody to Daredevil, Magneto and, only just, the Silver Surfer, and never to a character who is there. KEYWORD says it to Captain America (only just) and Daredevil, and also to Venom, Misty Knight and Mayday Parker, who are there. A threshold fitted on the clues it is then played on flatters the machines; on clues it was not fitted to, MEANING makes about 5.5 points an episode and KEYWORD 2.7.

After every clue comes the instant replay. KEYWORD's side shows the clue as the engine read it: each word shaded by what it added to KEYWORD's pick, underlined where it would have counted for the right page instead, struck through if no page uses it. MEANING's side shows the passage it matched and the right page's best passage, with the clue's words marked, so you can see how few of them there are. The episode of the day is the same for everyone and ends with a result to paste.

 What surprised us

  1. The Two-Gun Kid. A Western hero from 1948 is KEYWORD's favourite wrong answer, six times, because gun and kid are plain English and his name.
  2. Keyword misses are names even when the clue has none. Red, girl, bullet, bug, night, star, agent, woman: 32 of 58 misses hang on a word that is part of the wrong page's title.
  3. MEANING's paraphrase wins are its least confident answers. 15 of the 16 score below what it gave Hercules for Thor.
  4. Both engines fell for the Invaders. A homonym fools a bag of words and a sentence encoder alike.
  5. The most famous word is the weakest. spider is on 146 of 303 pages, so in Spider-Man's own clue it counts for almost nothing.
  6. Cutting a page at 256 pieces is not that bad, for the wrong reason. The lead paragraph is a summary, and clues in the page's own words echo it.
  7. The biggest encoder is surest about the wrong god. Asked about Thor, mxbai-embed-large answers Beta Ray Bill, the alien who was found worthy of his hammer, and that answer stands out from the crowd (z = 4.15) as far as its typical right answer does (median 4.18).

 What we checked in the text

  • Every clue's vocabulary against its page before the first ranking, and the two clues that still leak, named above.
  • All 45 passages behind MEANING's misses on other-words clues, labelled by us; the analysis reads the file back and stops if a label is for a clue or a pick that the ranking does not produce.
  • For every KEYWORD miss, the word that carried it, computed term by term, and the ten in the table above read against the pages.
  • The six passages MEANING returned for the missing characters, quoted in the table, and the Iron Man case it exposed.
  • The course's Russian spy example, as a check that our passages and encoder are the course's.
The limitation that matters. We wrote every clue, so "other words" means our other words: three students who know the pages and were deliberately avoiding their vocabulary. Real searchers paraphrase less, and a question typed into a search box is closer to our page-words clues, where the two engines tie. The numbers are also small: 61 pairs give a top-three count of 24 an uncertainty of about plus or minus 12 percentage points, and six missing characters show the problem, they do not measure it. The same goes for the NOBODY HERE rule, fitted on five of them: the leave-one-out numbers are the honest ones, and they are rough.

 Reproduce it

One module holds the two engines and the passage rule, one script builds every figure and number in this post, one builds the game's data, and one test recomputes the data from scratch and runs the game's rules.

git clone https://github.com/Oddvar112/Social-Graphs-and-Interactions
cd Social-Graphs-and-Interactions

python scripts/build_week7_show.py --check   # the vocabulary check of the clues (no model needed)
python scripts/analyse_week7.py              # every figure and number in this post (a minute the first time, then seconds)
python scripts/compare_week7_encoders.py     # six encoders on the same clues, and the NOBODY HERE rule -> data/week7_encoders.json
python scripts/build_week7_show.py           # rankings, evidence, lineups and nobody flags for the game -> data/week7_show.js
python scripts/test_in_other_words.py        # recomputes every ranking, passage and flag, runs the game's core.js under node

python -m http.server 8000                   # then open http://localhost:8000/weeks/week7/show/

Needs numpy, networkx, matplotlib, torch and transformers. The post's encoder is sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (90 MB) and the game's is mixedbread-ai/mxbai-embed-large-v1 (670 MB); the comparison downloads four more, about 2 GB in all, the first time. Each is loaded with plain transformers, safetensors weights only, and pooled and prefixed the way its model card says (SPECS in scripts/week7_text.py). Passage vectors are cached in .cache/. Every number above is written to data/stats_week7.json. The clues are in data/week7_clues.tsv, our reading of MEANING's misses in data/week7_meaning_misses.tsv. The game's test recomputes all 128 rankings under both of the game's engines, every stored passage, word contribution and standout measure, checks that every nobody flag follows the fitted rule and that the rule refits to the same thresholds, checks that every lineup holds the right page and both number ones, and plays the rules: an episode is ten clues, four, five and one, never the same character twice; NOBODY HERE scores only on a missing character, for you and for the machines alike. The text is the course's frozen release, marvel_pages.zip, unmodified. Character pictures are the lead images of the Wikipedia articles, loaded as you play; we store only their addresses.
